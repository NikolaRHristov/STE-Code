# ASD-STE100 — Introduction, History, and Usage

## What Is ASD-STE100?

ASD-STE100 (often called STE for short) is a **controlled natural language** and an **international standard** for writing technical documentation. It is fully owned by ASD — the Aerospace, Security and Defence Industries Association of Europe, based in Brussels, Belgium.

ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English is a set of **Writing Rules (Part 1)** and a **Dictionary of controlled vocabulary (Part 2)**. The Writing Rules cover aspects of grammar and style, while the Dictionary specifies the general words that are approved for use — words chosen for their simplicity and ease of recognition. A core principle is that there is generally **only one word for one meaning, and one part of speech for one word**, which reduces ambiguity. The dictionary contains approximately **3,000 approved words** (sometimes referenced as an ~800-word core vocabulary).

---

## Why It Was Created

STE was developed in the late 1970s by the European Association of Aerospace Industries (AECMA, now ASD), with support from the Aerospace Industries Association of America (AIA), at the request of European airlines. The goal was to make aircraft maintenance documentation easier to understand for readers with only a basic command of English.

The resulting AECMA Simplified English Guide was released in **1986**. In 2005 it became an international specification, and in **2025** it became an international standard: ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English.

---

## Where It Is Used

STE was initially applicable to commercial aviation and then became a requirement for Defence projects, including land and sea vehicles. In commercial aviation, since 1986, STE has been a requirement of the ATA Specification i2200 (formerly ATA100) and ATA104 (Training). STE is also a requirement of the **S1000D** Specification. The European Defence Standards Reference (EDSTAR) recommends STE as a best-practice standard for defense contracting across all EDA participating member states.

Today, STE is used by major aerospace and defense organizations including:
- Airbus
- Boeing
- Lockheed Martin
- BAE Systems
- NATO
- Honeywell
- Northrop Grumman
- Safran
- Textron Aviation
- Pilatus
- RTX

Beyond aerospace and defense, other industries increasingly want to use it for purposes beyond maintenance documentation, and interest is also growing in the academic world (engineering and linguistics).

## How It Works

The specification is maintained by the **ASD STEMG** (Simplified Technical English Maintenance Group), consisting of representatives from ASD member and non-member countries. The current issue is **Issue 8, dated April 2021** (with Issue 9 released January 2025). Although the STE structure is stable and consolidated, the language is kept in line with technology evolution and amended based on continuous user feedback.

The core value proposition is that **STE makes technical texts easy to understand by all readers** — it removes linguistic barriers, reduces human-factors risk, and ensures correct understanding of maintenance instructions by operators. Organizations implementing STE report an average **25% reduction in text volume**, which also slashes translation costs.

## Specification Structure

The document is divided into two main parts, plus front matter:

- **Front Matter**: Copyright statement, highlights section (listing all changes from the previous issue), table of contents, subject-to-rule index, and general introduction.
- **Part 1 – Writing Rules**: 53 rules organized across 9 sections.
- **Part 2 – Dictionary**: A controlled vocabulary with approved and unapproved words, each carrying a specific part of speech and meaning.

The general introduction clarifies that STE was developed in the late 1970s at the request of European airlines (80% of whose personnel were non-native English speakers), formally founded as the AECMA Simplified English Working Group on June 30, 1983, and first released in 1986.

### The Subject-to-Rule Index

The subject-to-rule index functions like a UML use-case map — it maps linguistic concepts (e.g., "Active voice," "Noun clusters," "Imperative," "Safety instructions") to specific rule numbers across all 9 sections. Every linguistic concern is traceable to an enforceable rule.

## Key Principles from the Rules

### One Word, One Meaning, One Part of Speech

For example, "test" is an approved noun but not an approved verb — you must write "Do the leak test of the system" instead of "Test the system for leaks". Similarly, "follow" means "come after," not "obey" — so you write "Obey the safety instructions" rather than "Follow the safety instructions".

### Synonyms Are Eliminated

STE uses "start" instead of "begin," "commence," "initiate," or "originate". This reduces ambiguity and simplifies translation.

### Technical Names and Technical Verbs Are Permitted

These extend beyond the core dictionary, organized into 19 categories ranging from parts information and vehicle names to IT/telephony terms and damage terms. For instance, "engine" is a technical name (category 1), while a word like "base" can be a technical name in one context (mathematical term) but an unapproved word in another (a surface).

---

## The Controlled Dictionary

The dictionary section lists approved words in **uppercase** and unapproved words in **lowercase**, with suggested alternatives. Each entry specifies the approved part of speech and meaning. The Issue 7 highlights show extensive changes from Issue 6 — words added (e.g., CONCENTRIC, DEDICATED, PERPENDICULAR), words deleted (e.g., APPARENT, COMPOUND, DEBRIS from its previous listing), and approved meanings revised for many entries. The dictionary is designed so that each word has a single, specific definition to prevent misinterpretation.

---

## Copyright and Access

The specification is fully owned by ASD, Brussels, and copyrighted — reproduction "in whole or in part" requires written authority from an ASD officer. However, "irrevocable permission to use" is granted to national ASD member associations, AIA/AIAC members, airlines, defense ministries, the US DoD, and airworthiness authorities. The official website at **asd-ste100.org** allows you to request a free copy of the current issue.

## Version History (Brief)

The specification has evolved through nine issues since its 1986 debut:

| Issue | Year | Key Changes |
|-------|------|-------------|
| Issue 1 | 1986 | First release as AECMA Simplified English Guide |
| Issue 4 | 2005 | Renamed to ASD-STE100, became international specification |
| Issue 6 | 2013 | Made free of charge |
| Issue 7 | Jan 2017 | Rules reduced from 65 to 53; full revision of writing rules; section titles changed |
| Issue 8 | Apr 2021 | Added decision flowchart for word approval |
| Issue 9 | Jan 2025 | Became international standard; "technical name" → "technical noun"; FAIR principles adopted; AI task team established |

Issue 10 is scheduled for 2028.
